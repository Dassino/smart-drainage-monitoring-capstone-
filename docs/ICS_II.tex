\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

Urban flooding remains a critical challenge in Nairobi, Kenya, where inadequate drainage infrastructure, reactive management practices, and the absence of real-time monitoring continue to cause loss of life and destruction of property. This study presents the design, development and evaluation of a Smart Drainage Monitoring and Flood Prediction System, built as a hardware-software prototype of two IoT sensor nodes, a machine-learning pipeline and a web-based monitoring dashboard.

Two ESP32 nodes, each with an ultrasonic water-level sensor, a flow sensor and MQ-series gas sensors, sent their readings over MQTT to a laptop, where they were stored in PostgreSQL. A three-stage machine-learning pipeline was developed. An Isolation Forest flagged deviations from a Normal baseline, a Random Forest classified the state of the drain as Normal, Blockage or Flood and raised the alert, and an LSTM forecaster of the water level and the time to the Critical level was evaluated and is shown on the dashboard as an experimental indicator. The data were recorded on the prototype: a Normal baseline of about 23 hours and ten staged trials, five of Blockage and five of Flood. The models were evaluated with leave-one-trial-out validation. The study followed a mixed exploratory and experimental research paradigm, with CRISP-DM for the pipeline and Agile Scrum for development.

The Random Forest detected all ten trials and named the correct class in all ten, classifying 81\% of the readings during events correctly, which met the 80\% target narrowly. It warned at least five minutes before the Critical level on three of the ten trials, with a median lead time of 4.2 minutes; five minutes was physically possible on only six trials. The Isolation Forest alone detected four trials and raised four false alerts, and the LSTM forecaster did not do better than simple baselines. The hypothesis was therefore only partially supported: the classification target was met, but the five-minute warning target was met in three of the ten trials. The system was a locally deployed laboratory prototype and was not hosted publicly. The results show the technical feasibility of low-cost drain monitoring under controlled conditions, and they do not show performance on a live Nairobi drain.

\section{Introduction}
\subsection{Background Information}

Flooding has become one of the major environmental and infrastructural challenges affecting many urban areas across the world, especially in rapidly growing cities where drainage systems are unable to adequately support the increasing population and urban development. In Kenya, heavy rainfall seasons frequently result in severe flooding that leads to destruction of roads, disruption of transport systems, displacement of residents, environmental pollution, and loss of lives. According to \citet{avery2024}, floods experienced in Kenya between March and April 2024 caused the deaths of at least 169 people, with Nairobi being among the most affected urban areas. Although flooding is a natural occurrence, its effects have become more severe due to poor urban planning, blocked drainage systems, encroachment into riparian areas, and inadequate maintenance of stormwater infrastructure.

Rapid urbanization in Nairobi has significantly increased pressure on existing drainage systems. As urban areas expand, natural land surfaces are increasingly replaced by buildings, roads, and pavements that prevent water infiltration into the soil, resulting in increased surface runoff during rainfall. Studies on urban resilience in Nairobi established that the growth of impermeable surfaces has directly contributed to increased urban flooding and drainage overload \citep{nyamumbo2025}. Informal settlements and densely populated urban regions are particularly vulnerable because they often lack proper drainage infrastructure and waste management systems. Solid waste disposal into drainage channels further contributes to blockage of water flow, causing stormwater to overflow into roads, residential areas, and business premises.

Current drainage management approaches in many urban areas within Kenya are largely reactive rather than preventive. Maintenance activities are commonly performed after flooding has already occurred, making it difficult for authorities to effectively respond to emerging drainage issues before they escalate into disasters. The Nairobi Integrated Urban Development Master Plan (NIUPLAN) highlights that Nairobi’s drainage and sewerage systems have not kept pace with rapid urban growth and are increasingly unable to manage heavy stormwater flows effectively \citep{jica2014}.

\subsection{Problem Statement}

Urban flooding has become one of the most persistent and damaging challenges facing rapidly growing cities in Kenya, particularly Nairobi, where inadequate drainage infrastructure, poor waste management practices, and unplanned urban expansion continue to overwhelm existing stormwater systems. Despite the severity and frequency of flooding events in Nairobi, drainage management within the city remains largely reactive, relying on manual inspections and maintenance activities that are only carried out after flooding has already occurred. This means that county authorities and disaster management agencies are consistently unable to detect and respond to drainage problems before they escalate into full flooding disasters.

A major contributor to urban flooding in Nairobi is the blockage of drainage channels caused by the disposal of solid waste into drainage networks. As waste accumulates within drainage channels, water flow is gradually restricted, causing stormwater to overflow into roads, residential areas, and business premises during heavy rainfall. Currently, there are no automated systems in place to continuously monitor flow conditions within drainage channels and detect early signs of blockage before overflow occurs. As a result, blockages are typically only identified after they have already caused flooding, making timely intervention difficult for maintenance teams.

Beyond the challenge of physical blockages and flooding, urban drainage channels also present a serious but often overlooked environmental and public health hazard. The decomposition of organic waste materials that accumulate within drainage systems generates harmful gases including methane, hydrogen sulfide, and ammonia. These gases can accumulate within enclosed or partially enclosed drainage channels to dangerous concentrations, posing significant health risks to residents living near drainage infrastructure and to maintenance personnel working within or around drainage channels. Existing drainage systems in Kenya lack any mechanism for continuously monitoring the presence and concentration of such gases, leaving both the public and workers exposed to potential harm without warning.

Furthermore, the absence of data-driven tools for analyzing drainage conditions means that there is currently no reliable way to predict flooding events before they occur. Flood prediction requires continuous collection and analysis of data on water levels and flow rates across multiple points within a drainage network. Without such data, emergency response agencies are forced to act on flooding only after it has already caused damage, rather than being able to issue early warnings and take preventive action in advance.

Therefore, there exists a clear need for an intelligent drainage monitoring system capable of continuously collecting real-time data on water levels, water flow rates, drainage blockages, and harmful gas concentrations within urban drainage channels. Such a system should be capable of analyzing collected sensor data using machine learning techniques to detect anomalies, identify possible blockages, predict potential flooding events, and generate timely early warning alerts for county authorities and disaster management agencies in Kenya.

\subsection{Justification}

Urban flooding continues to cause significant loss of life, destruction of infrastructure, displacement of residents, and disruption of economic activities in Nairobi and other urban areas across Kenya. Existing approaches to drainage management are largely manual, reactive, and heavily dependent on physical inspections carried out after flooding has already occurred. These approaches are insufficient given the scale, speed, and frequency of flooding events experienced in rapidly urbanizing areas. A proactive, technology-driven solution is therefore necessary to bridge the gap between the current state of drainage management and the level of responsiveness required to protect lives and property effectively.

The smart drainage monitoring and flood prediction system developed in this project addresses this gap by monitoring drainage conditions continuously at several points along a drainage channel. Unlike manual inspection, the system operates without physical presence, so that drainage anomalies and possible blockages can be detected at any time of day. This continuous monitoring is intended to reduce the time between the start of a drainage problem and the response by maintenance teams or emergency agencies, a benefit this study evaluates within the controlled prototype described in Chapter~\ref{sec:ch3}, and does not demonstrate on a live drainage network.

The multi-model machine learning pipeline distinguishes the system from basic sensor-based monitoring that only reports raw readings. It has three stages. The first, an anomaly detector, monitors incoming readings and flags significant deviations from the established baseline. The second, a Random Forest, analyses the readings of both nodes and classifies the drainage state as Normal, Blockage or Flood, giving authorities a clear status and raising the alert. The third, an LSTM, was meant to forecast the water level and the time remaining before the Critical level, so as to give lead time for public warnings and maintenance; it was evaluated as a forecaster, and Chapter~5 shows how far it achieved this.

The three-stage pipeline of anomaly detection, drainage state classification and flood prediction was chosen because of the distinct and complementary role that each technique plays. Anomaly detection serves as the first line of analysis, continuously monitoring incoming sensor streams and flagging deviations from established baseline conditions before any threshold is breached, which reduces the risk of missed detections that purely threshold-based systems are prone to \citep{salim2024}. Random Forest classification then provides a computationally efficient and interpretable drainage state assessment that is well suited to the noise and variability inherent in sensor data collected from drainage environments \citep{sathiyamoorthy2025}. The LSTM component was meant to address the temporal dimension of flood risk by learning sequential patterns in water level and flow rate trends and estimating the warning time before critical conditions are reached \citep{bao2025}. Together, the three techniques were expected to let the system detect, classify and forecast drainage conditions at different time horizons, giving county authorities and disaster management agencies both immediate awareness and a forward-looking estimate.

Gas sensors were included to add a dimension of public health and occupational safety monitoring that existing drainage systems in Kenya lack. The prototype records the voltages of a methane sensor (both nodes) and an air-quality sensor (node~1). Because these sensors were not calibrated, the study does not claim to measure gas concentrations or to raise gas hazard alerts, although continuous gas monitoring could reduce the risk of exposure-related incidents if validated and deployed at scale.

The use of ESP32 microcontrollers, MQTT communication protocols, and a web-based monitoring dashboard makes the system relatively affordable and scalable compared to large-scale drainage infrastructure redevelopment projects. The system's architecture suggests potential for incremental deployment across additional drainage points as resources allow, though this study establishes only the technical feasibility of that architecture at the prototype scale, and not its practical readiness for county-level deployment. It also aligns well with smart city development initiatives being pursued within Nairobi and other urban centers in Kenya.


\subsection{Scope and Limitations}
\subsubsection{Scope}
\label{sec:scope}

This study focused on the design and development of a prototype smart drainage monitoring and flood prediction system for urban drainage channels in Kenya. Two sensor nodes were built around ESP32 microcontrollers to monitor the water level, the water flow rate and gas sensor voltages (methane at both nodes and air quality at node~1) within a channel model. A possible blockage was detected by comparing the readings of the upstream and the downstream node. Under normal conditions the flow at adjacent nodes stays in a consistent range, and a sustained drop in flow at the downstream node relative to the upstream node, with the water level rising upstream, was treated as a sign of a blockage between the two points. Readings were sent to a central platform over MQTT.

The study also developed a multi-model machine learning pipeline for drainage data analysis, anomaly detection, classification and flood prediction, which combines three techniques. An Isolation Forest flagged significant deviations from normal conditions. A Random Forest took the readings of both nodes and classified the state as Normal, Blockage or Flood, and its prediction raised the alert. A Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network was evaluated as a forecaster of the water level and the time to the Critical level. The outputs of the first two stages are presented on a web-based dashboard for real-time monitoring, and stored trials can be replayed.
\clearpage
The study did not involve physical reconstruction or redesign of existing drainage infrastructure, and did not use camera-based monitoring or computer vision. The system focuses on urban drainage monitoring and does not extend to river flooding, landslides or any other disaster outside urban drainage networks. The system remained at the prototype and testing phase, without nationwide or large-scale deployment.

The three machine learning models address different tasks with different data needs. The Isolation Forest performs unsupervised anomaly detection on unlabelled Normal readings. The Random Forest performs supervised multi-class classification and needs a labelled dataset of all three drainage states. The LSTM performs sequence prediction and needs continuous time-series data recorded at regular intervals. Each model was developed and evaluated through its own CRISP-DM cycle (Section~\ref{sec:crisp-dm}) before the pipeline was assembled, using data prepared separately for each model (Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}).

\subsubsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The development of the system was affected by several foreseeable challenges. The anomaly detector needed a reliable baseline of normal drainage conditions before it could flag deviations. Only about 23 of the 48 hours planned for the baseline were recorded, which carries a risk of false alerts, where normal variation is flagged as an anomaly, and of missed anomalies, which reduces the reliability of the detector.

The LSTM presented a particularly significant data challenge. LSTM models need large volumes of historical time-series data to train and to give reliable predictions, whereas the prototype generated its own data in a controlled environment, ten staged trials in all, which limited the accuracy of the forecasts. The plan to supplement these data with public datasets (Section~\ref{sec:data-acquisition}) was not followed, because those datasets could not be matched to the sensors and conditions of the prototype.

The Random Forest needed a labelled dataset in which drainage conditions were categorised as Normal, Blockage or Flood. Because the experimenter assigned the labels by hand when each condition was staged, some subjectivity may have entered the class boundaries, which could affect the consistency of the classifications.

Beyond the machine learning components, the sensor nodes were exposed to moisture and variable flow rates during testing, and the flow and gas sensors were not calibrated. Transmission of data between the nodes and the central platform depended on a stable wireless connection, and interruptions affect the continuity of real-time monitoring. Power outages or fluctuations could also interrupt a node and the collection of data. Because the system was tested as a prototype in a controlled environment, real-world drainage conditions such as physical waste accumulation, chemical exposure and variable channel flow were not fully replicated, which may limit how far the results apply to a live drain.

Most importantly, this system was developed and evaluated strictly as a prototype. All training and evaluation data originated from a physical prototype comprising two sensor nodes operating in a controlled simulation rig, not from a real drainage network. Consequently, the system has \emph{not} been validated on actual Nairobi drainage infrastructure, and its performance under real field conditions including sensor drift over extended deployment, unreliable wireless connectivity, extreme weather, debris and sediment behaviour distinct from the prototype's controlled water simulation, and the physical geometry of real drainage channels and manholes remains untested. The results presented in this study should be interpreted as evidence of technical feasibility under controlled conditions, and any future field deployment would require a separate, dedicated validation study beyond the scope of this project.
 
The staged trials used to generate labelled training and evaluation data (Section~\ref{sec:labelling-protocol}) relied on manual intervention -- obstructing the channel by hand or with a repositioned length of pipe, and increasing inflow by manually adding water -- rather than automated or electronically calibrated equipment such as a solenoid valve or a controllable pump. This introduces a degree of inconsistency in the exact severity of each staged Blockage or Flood trial from one repetition to the next, since the precise obstruction depth or rate of added water cannot be reproduced with the same precision that calibrated equipment would allow.


\subsubsection{Delimitations}

This study was limited to urban drainage channels in Nairobi, Kenya, and excluded rural drainage systems, river systems and drainage networks outside urban areas. The system focuses on drainage-related flooding, blockage detection within drainage channels and the recording of gas readings from waste decomposition, and does not extend to other forms of environmental or disaster monitoring such as landslide detection, wildfire monitoring or weather forecasting.

In terms of machine learning scope, the study implemented a three-stage pipeline of anomaly detection, Random Forest classification and LSTM-based forecasting, and did not explore other machine learning techniques or deep learning architectures. The models were trained on data recorded on the prototype during controlled testing (Section~\ref{sec:data-acquisition}), and their performance was evaluated only within the prototype testing phase and was not benchmarked against large-scale real-world flood prediction systems.

The system was developed with ESP32 microcontrollers, MQTT and web technologies, and the study did not explore other hardware platforms or communication protocols. The prototype was limited to two sensor nodes in one channel model, which was enough to validate the core functionality of the system.

\section{Literature Review}
\subsection{Introduction}

This chapter reviews existing literature relevant to the development of a smart drainage monitoring and flood prediction system for urban areas in Kenya. The chapter begins by examining the challenges associated with existing urban drainage monitoring and flood management systems, drawing from recent studies to establish the context within which this project is situated. It then reviews related systems and machine learning implementations that are relevant to the solution, analysing their strengths and limitations in the context of the objectives defined in Chapter 1. The chapter concludes with an identification of the gaps in existing solutions and a conceptual framework that illustrates the expected inputs, processes, outputs, and feedback mechanisms of the system.

\subsection{Challenges Associated with Existing Urban Drainage Management Practices}

Urban drainage monitoring and flood management remain among the most persistent infrastructure challenges in rapidly growing cities across the developing world. This section examines the key challenges that define the current state of drainage management, drawing from recent research to substantiate the need for a smarter and more proactive approach to managing urban drainage systems in Kenya and similar contexts.

\subsubsection{Reactive and Manual Drainage Management Practices}

One of the most widely documented challenges in existing urban drainage management is the heavy reliance on reactive and manual maintenance practices. \citet{nageye2025} observe that traditional flood management methods, which rely heavily on manual intervention, are inadequate in resource-constrained urban environments due to a lack of real-time monitoring capabilities. This observation is directly applicable to the Kenyan context, where county authorities typically respond to drainage failures only after flooding has already occurred. The consequence of this reactive approach is that drainage blockages are rarely detected early enough to prevent overflow, and maintenance teams are deployed in response to damage rather than in prevention of it. \citet{kumar2025} similarly note that existing flood monitoring systems are often expensive, complex, and require manual intervention, further limiting their practical utility in rapidly urbanizing areas with constrained public resources.

The absence of automated, continuous monitoring means that drainage conditions between inspection cycles are entirely unknown to management authorities. \citet{sonawane2018} highlight that not all areas have drainage monitoring teams, leading to irregular monitoring of drainage conditions, which in turn contributes to blockages that eventually trigger flooding. This irregular monitoring is particularly problematic in densely populated informal settlements where drainage networks are most susceptible to blockage from solid waste accumulation and where the consequences of flooding are most severe.

\subsubsection{Inadequate Blockage Detection Mechanisms}

A closely related challenge is the lack of automated mechanisms for detecting drainage blockages before they cause overflow. \citet{sonawane2018} point out that today's drainage systems are not computerised, making it difficult to know whether a blockage is occurring at a particular location. Without flow monitoring across multiple drainage points, blockages can develop gradually and remain undetected until water overflow has already begun. \citet{nageye2025} reinforce this by noting that most existing smart drainage systems primarily focus on flood detection and passive monitoring using sensors without providing actionable insights or response mechanisms, and that prior studies often lack real-time professional dashboards that provide decision-makers with detailed reasons for imminent flooding at specific locations.

The inability to detect blockages early has direct consequences for flood severity. When a blockage goes undetected, stormwater that would otherwise flow freely accumulates upstream of the obstruction, rapidly increasing water levels and dramatically shortening the time available for emergency response. This challenge is compounded in urban areas like Nairobi where drainage channels frequently receive solid waste from surrounding settlements, making blockages a routine rather than exceptional occurrence.

\subsubsection{Absence of Harmful Gas Monitoring}

Beyond physical blockages and flooding, existing drainage systems present an environmental and public health hazard that is largely ignored in current management practices. The decomposition of organic waste materials within drainage channels generates harmful gases including methane, hydrogen sulfide, and ammonia, which can accumulate to dangerous concentrations within enclosed or partially enclosed drainage infrastructure. \citet{sonawane2018} explicitly identify this as a serious risk, noting that waste in drainage lines can produce various gases such as methane and carbon monoxide which are harmful and can cause serious problems if inhaled by humans in large amounts, and that these problems are generally faced by drainage workers, with death being a possible consequence. Despite this well-documented risk, the majority of existing drainage monitoring systems reviewed in the literature do not incorporate gas detection capabilities, leaving maintenance personnel and nearby residents without any form of hazard warning.

\subsubsection{Limited Data-Driven Flood Prediction Capability}

A further challenge in existing systems is the absence of data-driven analytical tools capable of predicting flood events before they occur. \citet{sathiyamoorthy2025} observe that existing frameworks for flood management involve the use of static databases and non-integrated methods of data analysis that may not support real-time response mechanisms. Without the ability to analyse trends in sensor data and generate predictive alerts, emergency response agencies are forced to react to flooding after the fact rather than taking preventive action ahead of time. \citet{hiroi2016} similarly note that previous flood prediction methods have difficulty detecting complex water flow flooding with high accuracy because water level behaviour differs depending on a location's environment, highlighting the need for location-specific predictive models trained on locally collected sensor data.

\subsubsection{Connectivity, Power, and Scalability Constraints}

Finally, existing IoT-based drainage monitoring systems face practical deployment challenges related to power supply, network connectivity, and scalability. \citet{nageye2025} acknowledge that as IoT-based systems expand to cover more drainage points, challenges related to data management and system performance may arise, and that ensuring sustainable maintenance and cost-effectiveness is a key challenge in scaling such systems. \citet{kumar2025} note that geographical barriers and sensor competency limitations can affect system effectiveness, while connectivity issues in areas with limited network coverage remain a recurring constraint in deploying real-time monitoring systems in developing urban contexts. These challenges are directly relevant to the Kenyan urban environment, where power supply instability and intermittent network connectivity are common in many drainage-prone areas.

\subsubsection{Smart Real-Time Drainage Monitoring System Using Internet of Things}

\citet{sonawane2018} developed a smart real-time drainage monitoring system using IoT technology, targeting urban drainage networks in Nashik, India. The system was designed to address the challenges of manual drainage monitoring by deploying sensor nodes within manholes to monitor water levels, detect drainage blockages, and measure the concentration of harmful gases including methane, carbon monoxide, and sulfur dioxide. The system was primarily intended for use by municipal health departments and drainage maintenance personnel who required real-time information on drainage conditions to enable timely corrective action.

The hardware architecture of the system consisted of sensor nodes built around Arduino-based microcontrollers interfaced with gas sensors, water level sensors, and blockage detection sensors. Each node was connected to neighbouring nodes using NRF radio frequency communication modules, enabling data to hop from node to node across the drainage network before being transmitted to a cloud server via a gateway node equipped with a GSM or Wi-Fi module. The collected data was made accessible through a web-based interface that displayed live sensor readings and triggered alerts when threshold values were exceeded \citep{sonawane2018}.

\subsubsection{IoT-Based Smart Drainage System for Flood Management in Mogadishu, Somalia}

\citet{nageye2025} developed an IoT-based smart drainage system tailored to the flooding challenges of Mogadishu, Somalia, a context that shares several characteristics with Nairobi's urban drainage environment including inadequate infrastructure, reactive management practices, and recurrent flooding during heavy rainfall seasons. The system targeted municipal authorities and emergency management stakeholders who required real-time visibility into drainage conditions across the city's network of drainage collection points.

The system integrated ultrasonic HC-SR04 sensors for water level monitoring and YF-S201 Hall effect flow meters for measuring water flow rates at critical drainage collection points. Sensor data was transmitted via SIM800L GSM GPRS modules to an AWS cloud platform where it was processed and visualised through a web application built using Python Django. The system also incorporated solar-powered DC submersible water pumps that were automatically activated when water levels exceeded a predefined threshold, physically transporting excess water through HDPE pipes to the ocean to actively mitigate flooding rather than merely detecting it \citep{nageye2025}. Field testing across three high-risk flood zones in Mogadishu over six months demonstrated that the HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensors achieved a 98.7\% accuracy rate compared to manual water level measurements, with an average system response time of 3.2 seconds from threshold detection to alert generation.

\subsubsection{IoT-Based Flood Monitoring and Control System}

\citet{kumar2025} presented an IoT-based flood monitoring and control system designed to provide real-time flood detection, early warning generation, and automated flood control in both urban and rural flood-prone areas. The system targeted local authorities, emergency services, and residents in flood-prone zones who required timely alerts and automated responses to rising water levels. The system was explicitly designed to operate with minimal human intervention and without complex artificial intelligence algorithms, focusing instead on threshold-based decision logic to trigger alerts and automated control mechanisms such as floodgate activation \citep{kumar2025}.

The system utilised a network of water level sensors, rainfall gauges, soil moisture sensors, and temperature and humidity sensors connected to an Arduino UNO microcontroller interfaced with an ESP8266 Wi-Fi module for cloud connectivity. Sensor data was continuously compared against predefined threshold values, with the system generating SMS alerts and activating automated control responses when thresholds were exceeded. The system classified water levels into four zones: normal, yellow caution zone, red alert zone, and high alert flood zone, with corresponding alert messages and automated actions triggered at each level.

\subsubsection{Machine Learning Implementations for Flood Prediction and Anomaly Detection}

The integration of machine learning into flood monitoring and prediction systems has emerged as a significant area of research, offering capabilities that go beyond the threshold-based detection approaches of basic IoT systems. This section reviews three key machine learning studies relevant to the multi-model pipeline incorporating anomaly detection, Random Forest classification, and LSTM-based prediction.

The foundation of the machine learning pipeline is an anomaly detection component that continuously monitors incoming sensor readings and flags significant deviations from established baseline drainage conditions. \citet{sathiyamoorthy2025} emphasise that real-time data integration is a critical component of effective flood management systems, enabling response to environmental changes as they occur rather than after they have escalated (p.~2). The technical basis for using flow rate differentials as a blockage detection mechanism is further supported by \citet{ramesh2026}, who deploy flow rate sensors alongside water level sensors at multiple drainage monitoring nodes and use the deviation in flow readings between adjacent nodes as a primary feature in a blockage prediction model. Their system achieves a false alarm rate below 10\% compared to 15 to 30\% for static threshold-based systems, demonstrating that flow-based differential analysis is a more reliable approach to blockage detection than water level thresholds alone. \citet{nagarajan2025} similarly demonstrate that rising water level deviations detected between monitoring points serve as reliable indicators of blockage events within drainage pipelines, providing further support for the flow differential approach adopted in the system 

Random Forest classification is employed in the second stage of the pipeline to classify the current state of the drainage system as Normal, Blockage or Flood based on real-time sensor readings. \citet{sathiyamoorthy2025} describe Random Forest as a technique that works well with predicting long-term data and data containing noise and missing values, making it well suited for sensor data collected in harsh drainage environments where readings may be affected by physical debris, moisture, and chemical contamination. In their comparative evaluation of flood prediction algorithms, Random Forest achieved an accuracy of 91.4\% with a precision of 90.2\% and recall of 92.5\%, demonstrating strong performance in classifying flood-related conditions from multi-source environmental data. Importantly, Random Forest also demonstrated the lowest computational resource requirements among the models tested, with an average processing time of 12 milliseconds and CPU usage of 45\%, making it practical for deployment in resource-constrained embedded system environments.

The third stage of the pipeline employs a Long Short-Term Memory network to analyse historical sensor data trends and predict future water levels, estimating the time remaining before critical flood thresholds are reached. \citet{bao2025} demonstrate the application of LSTM networks for water depth prediction at multiple control points, showing that LSTM models trained on observed historical data can achieve Nash-Sutcliffe Efficiency values consistently above 0.98 and RMSE values ranging from 0.26 to 0.29 metres across multiple lead times, and that LSTM-based predictions could be generated for lead times of up to six hours, providing valuable advance warning of developing flood conditions. \citet{hiroi2016} similarly demonstrate that LSTM-based prediction trained on location-specific historical water level and rainfall data can detect complex water flow flooding five minutes earlier than actual water rising occurs, with a Root Mean Square Error of 2.8 centimetres under localised heavy rain conditions. \citet{banupriya2025} further support the use of LSTM networks for sequential sensor data analysis, noting that LSTM networks extract temporal dependencies and trends in rainfall, water levels, and soil displacement, and that combining LSTM with classification models produces more robust and accurate disaster prediction outcomes than using either approach in isolation.

\subsection{Gaps in Existing Systems and Machine Learning Implementations}

The review of existing drainage monitoring systems and machine learning implementations in Section 2.4 reveals several significant gaps that the smart drainage monitoring and flood prediction system seeks to address.

The most fundamental gap across all three reviewed IoT systems is the absence of machine learning and predictive analytics capability. \citet{sonawane2018}, \citet{nageye2025}, and \citet{kumar2025} all present systems that rely on threshold-based comparisons of current sensor readings to predefined static values. While this approach can detect when conditions have already deteriorated to a dangerous level, it cannot predict that deterioration before it occurs, leaving authorities with little or no lead time for preventive action. \citet{nageye2025} explicitly acknowledge this gap, noting that the lack of labelled data precluded the development of predictive models in their system and recommending that future research explore the inclusion of predictive analytics.

A second significant gap is the absence of harmful gas monitoring in the flood monitoring and IoT drainage systems reviewed. Only \citet{sonawane2018} incorporated gas sensing into their system design, and even then the system did not monitor ammonia, which is also generated through decomposition of organic waste in drainage channels. Neither the Somalia system by \citet{nageye2025} nor the flood monitoring system by \citet{kumar2025} incorporated any form of gas detection, meaning that the public health and occupational safety dimension of drainage management remains entirely unaddressed in these systems.

A third gap relates to the integration of multi-stage machine learning pipelines that combine anomaly detection, classification, and prediction into a single cohesive system. The machine learning studies reviewed by \citet{sathiyamoorthy2025}, \citet{bao2025}, and \citet{banupriya2025} each address individual components of the prediction problem in isolation. None of the reviewed studies presents a system that combines anomaly detection as an initial trigger, Random Forest classification for real-time drainage state assessment, and LSTM-based prediction for future water level forecasting within a single integrated pipeline applied to urban drainage sensor data. Research by \citet{gandhimathinathan2024} demonstrates that combining LSTM-based feature extraction with Isolation Forest anomaly scoring produces superior detection performance compared to either technique applied independently, achieving an overall accuracy of 98.2\% on industrial sensor data. Similarly, \citet{yogarajan2025} show that hybrid anomaly detection frameworks combining Isolation Forest with density-based clustering consistently outperform single-model approaches across multiple IoT sensor datasets, achieving accuracies above 97\% on environmental and meteorological sensor streams. These findings support the design decision in the system to pipeline anomaly detection, Random Forest classification, and LSTM prediction as complementary stages rather than treating any single technique as a complete solution.

Finally, none of the reviewed systems were designed or evaluated within the specific context of Kenyan urban drainage environments, where the combination of rapid urbanisation, informal settlements, solid waste accumulation in drainage channels, and inadequate stormwater infrastructure creates a unique and compounded set of drainage management challenges. The system developed in this project therefore seeks to address these gaps by developing a prototype that integrates real-time sensor monitoring, harmful gas detection, blockage identification, and a three-stage machine learning pipeline within the specific operational context of urban drainage management in Nairobi, Kenya.

The conceptual framework presented in this section provides a visual and descriptive representation of the intended inputs, processes, outputs, and feedback mechanisms of the smart drainage monitoring and flood prediction system. The framework illustrates how the system's components interact to transform raw sensor data into actionable information for county authorities and disaster management agencies.

     \centering

     \caption{Conceptual Framework of the Smart Drainage Monitoring and Flood Prediction System}
     \label{fig:conceptual_framework}
 \end{figure}

The input layer of the conceptual framework represents the physical data collection component of the system. Distributed sensor nodes equipped with water level sensors, flow rate sensors, and gas sensors continuously collect measurements from within urban drainage channels. These readings are transmitted in real time to the centralised processing platform using the MQTT communication protocol, which provides lightweight and reliable data transmission suited to the constrained network environments typical of urban drainage deployments.

The system processing layer represents the analytical core of the system and operates in three sequential stages. In the first stage, an anomaly detection algorithm continuously monitors incoming sensor readings against established baseline conditions and flags any significant deviations as indicators requiring further analysis. In the second stage, a Random Forest classification model evaluates the current sensor readings to classify the drainage state as Normal, Blockage or Flood, providing a clear and immediately actionable status update. In the third stage, an LSTM model analyses the historical trend of sensor readings leading up to the current moment to forecast future water levels and estimate the time remaining before the Critical level is reached. This framework guided the design; Section~\ref{sec:design-changes} records how the roles of the stages changed during implementation.

The output layer represents the information products delivered to system users through a web-based monitoring dashboard. Outputs include real-time drainage condition displays, early flood warning alerts with estimated time to critical threshold, blockage location alerts identifying the section of drainage channel where a flow rate discrepancy has been detected between two consecutive monitoring nodes, gas hazard alerts when harmful gas concentrations exceed safe thresholds, and historical analytical reports that support longer-term drainage planning and maintenance scheduling.

The actors in this framework are county authorities and disaster management agencies, who receive the system's outputs through the web-based dashboard and alert notifications. These actors are responsible for interpreting the system's alerts and reports, deploying maintenance teams to identified blockage locations, issuing public warnings when flood risk alerts are generated, and responding to gas hazard alerts by dispatching appropriate safety personnel. The feedback loop from these actors back to the system represents the maintenance actions and calibration updates that result from field investigations, which in turn improve the accuracy of the system's baseline models and classification thresholds over time.

\subsection{Introduction}

This chapter describes the methodology used to develop and evaluate the Smart Drainage Monitoring and Flood Prediction System. \subsection{Research Paradigm}
\label{sec:research-paradigm}

The project adopted a mixed paradigm that combines exploratory and experimental approaches at three layers. The first layer is the overarching research paradigm, which governed the whole study. The second is the Cross Industry Standard Process for Data Mining (CRISP-DM), which structured the development of the machine-learning pipeline. The third is Agile Scrum, which governed the software and hardware development.

The exploratory component was the review of urban drainage monitoring practices and systems in Kenya (Chapter~2), which identified the challenges and gaps that the system addresses and informed the design decisions. The experimental component was the design, construction and evaluation of the system: two sensor nodes were built and tested in a controlled prototype environment to generate sensor data, and the machine-learning pipeline was trained and evaluated against measurable metrics. This follows the approach commonly adopted in artificial-intelligence and IoT research, in which a functional prototype is built, tested under realistic conditions and evaluated on quantifiable outcomes \citep{sathiyamoorthy2025}.

In line with the experimental paradigm, the study tested the following hypothesis:

\textit{A machine-learning pipeline of Isolation Forest anomaly detection, Random Forest drainage-state classification and Long Short-Term Memory flood prediction, trained on data from the prototype, will achieve a classification accuracy of at least 80\% in identifying drainage states and give a warning at least five minutes before the Critical level, within a controlled prototype environment.}

The hypothesis was tested with the metrics of Section~\ref{sec:metrics}, and the outcome is reported in Chapter~5.

\subsubsection{Data Acquisition}
\label{sec:data-acquisition}

Data acquisition is the collection of raw data from real-world sources for training, validating and evaluating machine-learning models \citep{bao2025}. No organisational drainage data were available for this project. At the proposal stage, public datasets from the UCI Machine Learning Repository \citep{gas_sensor_array_drift_dataset_224,twin_gas_sensor_arrays_361}, Kaggle \citep{iot_tsensor_kaggle,sensor_data_joshi,flood_prediction_kaggle,urban_flood_risk_kaggle}, Zenodo \citep{zenodo_flood_quality} and river-discharge records \citep{grdc_data,glofas_era5} were identified as candidate sources. They were not used to train the final models, because they come from different sensors and settings and contain no ground-truth labels for the conditions staged on this prototype (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-plan}). \citet{zhou2025} note that real IoT data are often limited by noise, imbalance and poor coverage of rare conditions, which is why generating data on the prototype itself was needed.

All data were therefore recorded on the physical prototype: two ESP32 sensor nodes along a channel model, each publishing about one reading every 8.5 seconds. The data comprised a Normal-condition baseline of about 23 hours, and ten staged trials, five of Blockage and five of Flood, giving 2,439 time-aligned readings. The sensors were remounted on 24 October 2026, and the models of water depth were trained on data from the new mounting only. The dataset is described in Chapter~5.

\subsubsection{CRISP-DM Machine Learning Methodology}
\label{sec:crisp-dm}

CRISP-DM is an iterative methodology for data-science projects with six phases: Business Understanding, Data Understanding, Data Preparation, Modelling, Evaluation and Deployment \citep{wirth2000}. It was used to give each of the three models a structured process, because each has different data needs, training and evaluation criteria.

In Business Understanding, the aim of each model was defined in terms of the drainage problem: the Isolation Forest to detect deviations from the baseline readings that may indicate a developing blockage or flood, the Random Forest to classify the state as Normal, Blockage or Flood, and the LSTM to forecast the water level and the time to the Critical level. In Data Understanding, the prototype data were examined for missing values, spikes and class imbalance. Data Preparation is described in Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}, Modelling in Section~\ref{sec:model-training}, and Evaluation in Section~\ref{sec:metrics}. A model that did not reach the performance needed was returned to Modelling or Data Preparation, which is how the Isolation Forest gate and the LSTM role were revised (Chapter~5). In Deployment, the Isolation Forest and the Random Forest were connected to the backend and the dashboard, and the LSTM was kept as an offline evaluation.

\subsubsection{Data Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocessing}

Raw sensor data from a drain contain gaps caused by lost connections, spikes caused by splashing and debris, and scale differences between sensors. These were handled in data preparation, with the raw readings kept in the database. A missing sensor value was sent by the firmware as $-1$ and treated as a gap. Spikes in the ultrasonic reading were smoothed with a rolling median of 5 readings, and the water depth was calculated from the distance measured to the dry floor of each node (45.76~cm at node~1 and 45.80~cm at node~2). Features were then computed over a sliding window. For each sensor the model used the current value and its mean, standard deviation and change over the last 7 readings, and for the water depth also the change over the last 24 readings (about 3.4 minutes), which picks up slow rises. The readings of node~2 were matched to those of node~1 by time, within 10 seconds, so that the Random Forest received 30 features from both nodes. The LSTM used windows of 24 readings.

The data were not split at random into training, validation and test sets in an 80-10-10 ratio. Neighbouring readings from one staged trial are strongly correlated, so a random split would place almost identical readings in both the training and the test set and overstate the performance. Every model was therefore evaluated by leave-one-trial-out validation: each of the ten trials was held out in turn while the models were trained on the other nine.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Plan}
\label{sec:evaluation-plan}

Evaluating the pipeline needs data whose ground truth is known with certainty. Public datasets cannot provide this for the sensors and drainage setting of this system (Section~\ref{sec:data-acquisition}), so evaluation data were generated on the prototype under two categories of controlled conditions.

The first category is the \textbf{Normal-condition baseline}: the prototype was run with the channel unobstructed and no extra inflow, so that the readings established the normal range of water level, flow rate and gas voltage. About 23 hours were recorded, against the 48 hours planned. The baseline was the only data used to train the Isolation Forest.

The second category is the set of \textbf{staged trials}, in which the condition was created deliberately by obstructing the channel and adjusting the water added. Because the experimenter sets the condition, the label of each trial was known when it was run and was not inferred afterwards from the readings. The trials and their labels are described in Section~\ref{sec:labelling-protocol}. The staged trials were not used to train the Isolation Forest; they were held out to evaluate it (Figure~\ref{fig:threshold-split}).

\subsubsection{Labelling Protocol for Drainage State Classification}
\label{sec:labelling-protocol}

The Random Forest is a supervised model and needs a labelled example of every state it recognises. To avoid a model that only reproduces a threshold rule used to make the labels, each label was assigned by the experimenter when the condition was staged. The two nodes were placed along the channel with a section between them that could be obstructed by hand or with a repositioned length of pipe, so that restricting the flow produced a flow difference between the upstream and the downstream node, as in the blockage detection approach of Section~\ref{sec:scope}.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal:} the water level and flow rate at both nodes stayed within the baseline range, the flow at the upstream and downstream nodes agreed, and no obstruction was applied.
    \item \textbf{Blockage:} the channel between the nodes was obstructed, with the obstruction kept as similar as possible across trials. This produced a sustained drop in flow at the downstream node relative to the upstream node, while the water level at the upstream node rose above its baseline range. Every reading during the obstruction was labelled Blockage.
    \item \textbf{Flood:} the inflow was increased by adding water faster than normal with a water pump and a bucket (Figure~\ref{fig:rig}) while the channel remained obstructed. Every reading during this condition was labelled Flood.
\end{itemize}

\noindent For each trial the operator logged three events with timestamps through the dashboard: the start of the event (onset), the moment the water reached the Critical level, and the end. The same staged trials served as the held-out evaluation data once the models were trained. Because the obstruction and the added water were applied by hand, the severity of each trial varied somewhat, which is a limitation noted in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsubsection{Model Training}
\label{sec:model-training}

Model training exposes an algorithm to a training dataset so that it learns patterns that it can apply to new data. Three models were trained, each with a formulation suited to its role. The Isolation Forest detects anomalies by isolating observations through random recursive partitioning of the feature space. For an observation $x$, the anomaly score $AS(x)$ is:

\begin{equation}
AS(x) = 2^{-\frac{E(h(x))}{c(n)}}
\end{equation}
\myequations{Isolation Forest Anomaly Score}

where $E(h(x))$ is the expected path length of $x$ across all isolation trees, $c(n) = 2H(n-1) - \frac{2(n-1)}{n}$ is the average path length of an unsuccessful search in a binary search tree, $H(i)$ is the harmonic number and $n$ is the number of training samples \citep{salim2024}. Scores near 1 indicate anomalies and scores near 0.5 indicate normal observations.

One Isolation Forest was fitted per node, strictly unsupervised, on the readings of the Normal baseline recorded after the new mounting only, without labels. No staged Blockage or Flood data were used in training. The labelled staged trials were used afterwards as ground truth to check when the detector flagged readings, without ever having been used to train it. Figure~\ref{fig:threshold-split} shows this separation.

\end{figure}

\paragraph{Anomaly threshold.}
The threshold was the 95th percentile of the anomaly scores of the baseline readings, and an alert was raised when 6 consecutive readings were flagged. The resulting detection and false alerts are reported in Chapter~5.

The Random Forest classifies the state by combining the predictions of $T$ decision trees. For an input vector $\mathbf{x}$ of window features, each tree $t$ gives a class $\hat{y}_t(\mathbf{x})$ and the final class is the majority vote:

\begin{equation}
\hat{y}(\mathbf{x}) = \arg\max_{c} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbf{1}[\hat{y}_t(\mathbf{x}) = c]
\end{equation}
\myequations{Random Forest Classification by Majority Vote}

where $c \in \{\text{Normal}, \text{Blockage}, \text{Flood}\}$ and $\mathbf{1}[\cdot]$ is the indicator function \citep{sathiyamoorthy2025}. Each tree is trained on a bootstrapped sample of the training data with a random subset of features at each split, which reduces variance on noisy sensor data. The model used 300 trees, a minimum of 3 samples per leaf, balanced class weights within each bootstrap sample, and a fixed random seed.

The LSTM is a recurrent network that learns temporal dependencies in sequences. At each time step $t$ its unit processes the input $\mathbf{x}_t$ through four gating mechanisms: the forget gate $f_t$, the input gate $i_t$, the candidate cell state $\tilde{c}_t$ and the output gate $o_t$, which update the cell state $c_t$ and the hidden state $h_t$:

\begin{equation}
f_t = \sigma(W_f \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_f)
\end{equation}
\myequations{LSTM Forget Gate}

\begin{equation}
i_t = \sigma(W_i \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_i)
\end{equation}
\myequations{LSTM Input Gate}

\begin{equation}
\tilde{c}_t = \tanh(W_c \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_c)
\end{equation}
\myequations{LSTM Candidate Cell State}

\begin{equation}
c_t = f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde{c}_t
\end{equation}
\myequations{LSTM Cell State Update}

\begin{equation}
o_t = \sigma(W_o \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_o)
\end{equation}
\myequations{LSTM Output Gate}

\begin{equation}
h_t = o_t \odot \tanh(c_t)
\end{equation}
\myequations{LSTM Hidden State}

where $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function, $W_f, W_i, W_c, W_o$ are weight matrices, $b_f, b_i, b_c, b_o$ are bias vectors and $\odot$ is element-wise multiplication \citep{bao2025}. The network had one LSTM layer of 32 units followed by dropout of 0.2, and it was trained with the Adam optimiser (learning rate 0.005, batch size 64, 40 epochs). It was used in two ways. As a classifier it predicted the state of the drain. As a forecaster it had two outputs, the change in water depth and the time to the Critical level, and it was run with three random seeds.

The Isolation Forest and Random Forest were implemented with scikit-learn, and the LSTM with PyTorch on the central processor. 

\subsubsection{Model Validation and Testing}
\label{sec:validation}

Validation and testing assess a model on data it was not trained on. With leave-one-trial-out validation, the readings of the held-out trial were never seen in training, so each trial acted as a test set for the models trained on the others. The pipeline was also run on the staged trials as a replay of the live pipeline, so that the alert and its timing could be checked against the events logged by the operator. The minimum acceptable accuracy of the classifier was 80\%, in line with the objective in Section~\ref{sec:objectives}.

\subsubsection{Model Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

The metrics were chosen to suit each task. For the Isolation Forest, the number of trials in which it raised an alert and the number of false alerts raised before the event started were counted. A high detection rate with a low false-alarm rate is the aim, as reported by \citet{salim2024}, whose Extended Isolation Forest reached a recall of 0.9977 and a false-alarm rate of 0.0003 on industrial IoT data.

For the Random Forest, the share of readings during events given the correct state was computed, with precision, recall and F1-score across the three states, and the class given at the first alert was checked. The minimum accuracy was 80\%. \citet{sathiyamoorthy2025} report 91.4\% accuracy for Random Forest on a comparable multi-class flood-risk task, which is a reference only.

For early warning, a direct lead-time experiment was used, because error values alone do not show that a warning came in time. For each staged trial, the \emph{first alert} was the first alert at or after the onset, and the \emph{lead time} was the time from that alert to the first Critical press. An alert before the onset counted as a false alert. A lead time of five minutes was the target, but only where it was physically possible, that is, where the water reached the Critical level at least five minutes after the onset; this was true for 6 of the 10 trials (Chapter~5). Figure~\ref{fig:leadtime-eval} shows the procedure for one trial.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Images/LeadTime_Evaluation_Diagram.png}
    \caption{Lead-time evaluation procedure for a single staged trial}
    \label{fig:leadtime-eval}
\end{figure}

The number of trials with a warning, the number with a lead time of five minutes or more, and the median lead time were reported for each method. For the LSTM forecaster, the root mean square error of the depth forecast was compared with two baselines, ``stays as now'' and a straight-line extrapolation, and the mean absolute error of the time to Critical was compared with a constant guess. Published values from related work, for example \citet{jadhav2025} with a root mean square error of 3.12 and \citet{bao2025} with 0.26 to 0.29 metres, were treated as reference points for different settings and not as expected results for this prototype.

\subsection{Software Development Methodology}
\label{sec:agile}

A software development methodology is a framework of processes, practices and phases for planning, building, testing and delivering a system \citep{sommerville2016}. Agile Scrum was selected for the design and development of this system. Scrum organises the work into fixed-length sprints, each of which produces a working increment. Mr.~Ouma Kelvin acted as Product Owner, defining and prioritising requirements and reviewing each increment in supervision sessions. The student acted as Scrum Master and as the development team, responsible for the design, implementation and testing.

The work was organised into six sprints on the Strathmore academic calendar: Sprint~1, requirements and system design (April and May 2026); Sprint~2, sensor node construction and MQTT setup (August and September 2026); Sprint~3, data acquisition and preprocessing (September 2026); Sprint~4, machine-learning model development (September and October 2026); Sprint~5, dashboard development and integration (October to November 2026); and Sprint~6, testing, evaluation and documentation (November 2026), which was in progress at the time of writing. Agile suits IoT work because hardware and software must be integrated iteratively \citep{gouthaman2018}, and \citet{jusoh2019} found that all surveyed IoT development organisations used Agile methods, with Scrum the most widely used.

\subsubsection{Requirements Elicitation}

Requirements were derived from the problem statement and the literature review (Chapter~2) and were reviewed with the supervisor as Product Owner. They covered monitoring water level, flow rate and gas voltage in real time, detecting blockages through the flow difference between adjacent nodes, classifying the drainage state with a Random Forest, forecasting with an LSTM, and showing alerts on a web dashboard, together with qualities such as reliability, sensor accuracy, alert timeliness and dashboard accessibility. They are catalogued in Section~\ref{sec:requirements}.

\subsubsection{System Design}

The design phase translated the requirements into specifications for the sensor node hardware, the MQTT data transmission, the PostgreSQL database, the Flask backend and the dashboard, and the pipeline and its stages. The deliverables of this phase are presented in Chapter~\ref{sec:ch4}.

\subsubsection{Prototype Development}

The prototype phase built the first working version: two sensor nodes were assembled around ESP32 boards and the sensors, the MQTT link to the Mosquitto broker was set up, the ingestion program that stores readings in PostgreSQL was written, and the first version of the dashboard was built. This version was the base for the sensor integration and model development sprints.

\subsubsection{Sensor Integration}

Sensor integration connected the sensors and checked that their readings reached the database. The distance from each ultrasonic sensor to the dry channel floor was measured with a tape and used as the depth reference. The flow sensor used the conversion factor of its datasheet and was not calibrated against a measured volume, and the gas sensors were not calibrated against reference concentrations; the gas readings are therefore reported as voltages. These limits are recorded in Chapter~5.

\subsubsection{Machine Learning Model Development}

The Isolation Forest was trained first, on the Normal baseline, followed by the Random Forest on the labelled trials and then the LSTM on sequences of readings. The Isolation Forest and the Random Forest were connected to the backend so that stored readings are scored for the dashboard. The LSTM forecaster was evaluated offline and was later connected to the dashboard as an experimental forecast (Section~\ref{sec:dashboard}).

\subsubsection{Coding and Development}

The backend was written in Python with Flask, and the dashboard in React. The models used scikit-learn and PyTorch. PostgreSQL stored the readings, the label set for each node and the logged trial events. The dashboard shows current readings, the alert state, stored trials and the model settings.

\subsubsection{Testing and Deployment}

Testing evaluated the system against the requirements: the hardware and integration tests, the leave-one-trial-out evaluation of the models, and the recovery tests, which are reported in Chapter~5. Because the system is a prototype, deployment meant demonstrating it in a controlled setting on one laptop, not installing it in a live drain.

\subsection{System Development Tools and Techniques}
\label{sec:tools}

\subsubsection{Python}

Python was the main language for the backend and the machine-learning pipeline, because of its libraries for IoT data processing and machine learning, its readability for rapid prototyping, and its wide use in flood prediction and sensor data research \citep{banupriya2025}. It was used for the backend logic, the program that receives the MQTT messages, the model implementations and the database access.

\subsubsection{Flask}

Flask was used as the web framework of the backend. It is a lightweight Python microframework that provides the interface through which the dashboard receives readings, detector output, trials and model settings as JSON. Flask was chosen over Django because the prototype needed no built-in account system or administration site.

\subsubsection{React}

The dashboard was built in React 19 and Vite, which gives a responsive browser interface that updates from the Flask interface every three seconds.

\subsubsection{Scikit-learn and PyTorch}

Scikit-learn provided the Isolation Forest and the Random Forest, with tested implementations and utilities for features and evaluation \citep{sathiyamoorthy2025}. PyTorch was used for the LSTM, because it was available on the development computer and supports recurrent networks on time-series data \citep{bao2025}. The proposal named TensorFlow and Keras, and PyTorch replaced them.

\subsubsection{PostgreSQL}

PostgreSQL stored the sensor readings, the labels and the trial events. It was selected for its support for time-series queries, its time-zone aware timestamp type, its ACID compliance under writes from several nodes, and because it is open source and suits a prototype with a small budget.

\subsubsection{MQTT Protocol and Mosquitto}

MQTT is a lightweight publish-subscribe protocol for constrained devices and low-bandwidth networks. Each node published its readings to its own topic on the Mosquitto broker, and a bridge program subscribed to the topics and stored the readings in the database \citep{nageye2025}.

\subsubsection{ESP32 Microcontroller}

The ESP32 is a low-cost, low-power microcontroller with built-in Wi-Fi. It was programmed with the Arduino framework to read the ultrasonic, flow and gas sensors of each node and to publish the readings. Its built-in Wi-Fi removed the need for a separate communication module, which kept the cost and size of each node small \citep{jayapriya2025}.

This section lists the modules and documentation that the development produced, and states which of them were built in the prototype.

\subsubsection{System Documentation}

The system documentation comprises the system design descriptions, the sensor node wiring, the model training and evaluation logs, the repository with its instructions, and this report.

\subsubsection{Sensor Data Monitoring Module}

This module receives, stores and shows the readings of the two nodes. It gives a live view of the water level, flow rate and gas voltages, and keeps a timestamped history in PostgreSQL for analysis and model training. It was built.

\subsubsection{Blockage Detection Module}

This module detects a blockage between two nodes. In the prototype the Random Forest receives the features of both nodes side by side, so that it can learn the difference between the upstream and the downstream node, and the module gives the class Blockage when the model predicts it. This module was intended to compare gas readings with safety thresholds for methane, hydrogen sulphide and ammonia and to raise a gas hazard alert. This module applies the pipeline to the stored readings. The Isolation Forest and the Random Forest run for the dashboard, and the Random Forest raises the alert. The LSTM forecaster is also run on the newest readings and shown on the dashboard as an experimental forecast; it does not raise the alert or sound the siren.

\subsubsection{Alert and Notification Module}

This module shows the alert state, the class and the Isolation Forest flag of each node on the dashboard. Alert acknowledgement, severity levels and notification by message were not built.

\subsubsection{Reporting and Analytics Module}

This module replays stored trials with their readings, detector output and logged events, and exports the readings as a CSV file. Generated reports by period, node and type were not built.

\subsubsection{System Architecture}

The architecture shows the main parts of the system and how the data pass between them. It is organised into three tiers, which handle data collection, processing and presentation.

The sensing layer consists of two ESP32 sensor nodes placed along the monitored channel. Each node has an ultrasonic water-level sensor, a flow sensor and an MQ-4 methane sensor; node~1 also has an MQ-135 air-quality sensor. The gas sensors give a voltage, not a concentration, because they were not calibrated. Each node publishes its readings as JSON over MQTT through Wi-Fi.

The processing layer consists of the Mosquitto broker, a bridge program that stores each message in the PostgreSQL database, the Flask interface, and the machine-learning pipeline. The pipeline reads the stored readings, computes features over a window of readings, and passes them to the Isolation Forest and the Random Forest, whose outputs are served to the dashboard through the Flask interface. The saved models are loaded from files, so a retrained model can replace an old one without changing the application code.

The presentation layer is the web dashboard, which shows the latest readings, the alert state and class of each node, stored trials and the model settings.

Figure~\ref{fig:system_architecture} shows that the database is the point where the tiers meet: the nodes never talk to the dashboard directly, and the pipeline and the dashboard read from the same stored data. This keeps each part replaceable, and it keeps every reading available for later evaluation. The cost is that a database failure interrupts the whole chain, which is why requirement NFR-05 asks for retry and recovery.

\subsubsection{Class Diagram}

A class diagram shows the classes of a system, their attributes and methods, and the relationships between them \citep{booch2005}. It is the blueprint for the software and shows where each requirement is handled in code. The diagram contains nine classes. The \texttt{SensorNode} class represents a physical node and provides methods for registering it and updating its status. The \texttt{SensorReading} class represents one timestamped record sent by a node. The \texttt{AnomalyDetector}, \texttt{DrainageClassifier} and \texttt{FloodPredictor} classes represent the Isolation Forest, the Random Forest and the LSTM stages. The \texttt{Alert} class represents a notification generated by the system, the \texttt{User} class represents an account, and the \texttt{Report} class represents a generated report. The ninth class, \texttt{ModelConfiguration}, holds the settings of the three model classes (model type, threshold, version, training date and active status), so that they can be changed without changing the code.

Each node carried an ESP32 board connected to the ultrasonic sensor, the flow sensor and the gas sensors listed in Table~\ref{tab:env-iot}. The floor distances above were the ones used to train the models. On 8 November 2026 the dry channel was recorded again (trial \texttt{rest\_d3}, 205 readings at node~1 and 206 at node~2). The median distances were 45.69~cm and 45.46~cm, with standard deviations of 0.027~cm and 0.007~cm (Table~\ref{tab:iot-tests}, TC-02). The difference from the training values is 0.07~cm at node~1 and 0.34~cm at node~2, which is small against the critical depth of about 3~cm, but the models were not retrained, and this is recorded as a limitation. \pending{Confirm that the backend floor values were changed to match, or state that only the dashboard display uses them.}

\subsubsection{Firmware and Data Acquisition}

The firmware connected each board to the hotspot and the broker and then repeated the cycle of reading the sensors and publishing one message per cycle. The stored readings show a spacing of about 8.5 seconds between consecutive readings of a node, which sets the 24-reading window used by the models at about 3.4 minutes. Each message carried the node identifier, the ultrasonic distance converted to a water level, the flow rate in litres per minute, and the voltages of the gas sensors. Gas outputs are reported as voltages because they were not calibrated against a reference. Occasional spikes in the ultrasonic reading, caused by turbulence and splashing, were removed during data preparation and not in the firmware. Alert thresholds are not held in the firmware; the alerts are computed from the stored readings by the models.

\subsubsection{Communication and System Integration}

A reading travelled from the sensor through the ESP32 and the Wi-Fi hotspot to the Mosquitto broker, from the broker to the bridge program, which stored it in PostgreSQL, and from the database through the Flask interface to the dashboard, which refreshed every three seconds. The broker ran without authentication on the private hotspot, which is recorded as a limitation.

Two failures occurred during testing and showed how the system behaves. When PostgreSQL stopped accepting connections after a crash on 29 October 2026, the bridge printed ``Database not reachable'' and retried every five seconds, and when the backend was not running the dashboard reported that it could not reach the interface. Neither stopped the other components, and normal operation resumed after the database was restarted. The bridge subscribes with the default settings, which keep no persistent session at the broker, so a gap in the stored readings is expected whenever the bridge is not running.

\subsection{Dataset Description and Preparation}
\label{sec:dataset}

\subsubsection{Data Source and Record Structure}

All data were generated on the physical prototype: two ESP32 sensor nodes placed along a drainage channel model, publishing over MQTT to a Mosquitto broker on a laptop hotspot. A bridge program stored each message in PostgreSQL. A record contained a node identifier, timestamp, ultrasonic water level (cm), flow rate (L/min), and the MQ-4 and MQ-135 gas sensor voltages. Node~1 recorded all four sensors; node~2 recorded depth, flow and MQ-4. Water depth was derived as $D_{floor} - d$, where $d$ is the ultrasonic distance and $D_{floor}$ is the distance to the dry channel floor, measured as 45.76~cm at node~1 and 45.80~cm at node~2 after the sensors were remounted on 24 October 2026.

Gas sensors were not calibrated against a reference instrument, so all models use the sensor output voltages and not concentrations in ppm. This is a deviation from the design, which assumed ppm values.

\subsubsection{Labelling by Event Times}

Labels were not assigned to the stored readings directly. During each staged trial, the experimenter recorded event times in a \texttt{trial\_events} table: the onset of the condition, one or more presses of a Critical marker, and the end of the trial. A reading was labelled \emph{Normal} before the onset; from the onset to the first Critical press it was labelled with the staged condition (\emph{Blockage} or \emph{Flood}); and from the first Critical press to the end of the trial it kept the staged label. The first five minutes after the end of a trial, while the water drained, were left unlabelled. Normal baseline and Normal-with-flow runs were labelled \emph{Normal}. The Critical marker represents the physical moment at which flooding was about to occur, judged by eye at node~1. Several presses per trial were recorded deliberately, to compare the critical depth across trials; the first press is the reference for lead time.

\subsubsection{Dataset Summary}

The final labelled set contains ten staged trials, five Blockage and five Flood, recorded on the new sensor mounting, together with Normal runs of the channel with and without flow. After the readings of the two nodes were matched to within 10 seconds, the evaluation set contained 2{,}439 time-aligned readings. The Normal data comprised 1{,}869 readings at node~1 and 1{,}805 at node~2. Readings from the earlier mounting (about 9{,}800 per node) trained the first versions of the models and were not used for the final depth models, because the two mountings produce different depth readings under flow.

Table~\ref{tab:critical-depth} summarises the critical depth at node~1 and the time from onset to the first Critical press. The latter matters for the lead-time objective: in only six of the ten trials did the event take five minutes or longer to reach the Critical level, so a five-minute warning was physically possible in only those six.

Depth at node~2 was unreliable while water was poured, showing bimodal values of about 44 and 47~cm and some negative depths. The depth at node~1 was therefore the main level signal, and node~2 mainly contributed its flow rate and gas readings; the Random Forest ranked the node~2 flow rate as its most important feature.

\subsubsection{Preparation and Partitioning}

Readings were cleaned and the ultrasonic depth was de-spiked before feature extraction. The preparation script (\texttt{ml/build\_dataset.py}) combined the exports of the three recording days, applied the event labels, excluded set-up and pilot trials, and wrote the labelled dataset. For the Random Forest, each reading was described by 30 features: depth, flow and MQ-4 (and MQ-135 at node~1) for both nodes, each with a 7-reading rolling mean, standard deviation and change, plus a 24-reading depth change. Readings of node~2 were matched to node~1 within 10 seconds.

Readings within a trial are strongly correlated, so a random split into training, validation and test subsets would place neighbouring readings in different subsets and overstate performance. The evaluation therefore used \emph{leave-one-trial-out} validation: each of the ten trials was predicted by models trained on the other nine trials plus the Normal data. This replaces the 80-10-10 split planned in Chapter~3, and it keeps every reading of the tested trial out of training.

\subsection{Model Implementation and Training}
\label{sec:models}

\subsubsection{Stage 1: Isolation Forest}

One Isolation Forest was trained per node on Normal readings from the new mounting only, using depth, flow and gas features over a rolling window. The alert threshold was the 95th percentile of the scores of the Normal training readings at both nodes, and an alert required six consecutive flagged readings, about 50 seconds. Training the node~2 model on both mountings flagged 38\% of its Normal readings from the new mounting, so the final models used the new mounting only. A flow-corrected depth variant was tested and gave no benefit.

\subsubsection{Stage 2: Random Forest}

The Random Forest classified each reading as Normal, Blockage or Flood from the 30 features above. It used 300 trees, a minimum of three samples per leaf, balanced subsample class weights and a fixed random seed. An alert required six consecutive non-Normal predictions. In the dashboard, the Random Forest diagnosis is the main alert, and the Isolation Forest status is shown beside it.

\subsubsection{Stage 3: LSTM}

Two LSTM variants were implemented in PyTorch (CPU), each with one layer of 32 hidden units, a dropout of 0.2, the Adam optimiser at a learning rate of 0.005, batches of 64 and 40 epochs. Both read windows of 24 consecutive readings (about 3.4 minutes) of seven channels: depth, flow and MQ-4 of both nodes and MQ-135 of node~1.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{LSTM classifier.} Each window was classified as Normal, Blockage or Flood using a class-weighted cross-entropy loss. This variant was evaluated only and is not part of the dashboard.
\item \textbf{LSTM forecaster (Stage 3 as designed).} The network predicted the change in node~1 depth five minutes ahead and the time remaining before the Critical level. Depth was smoothed with a causal median filter, and the Stage~2 class probabilities were added as inputs, as the design in Chapter~3 specified. Probabilities for training trials were obtained out-of-fold, so that no reading was scored by a forest that had seen its own trial. The final forecaster averaged three random seeds. A warning was raised when the forecast depth reached the median critical depth of the training trials (3.0~cm) for six readings in a row. The saved forecaster is also loaded by the backend and applied to the newest readings of both nodes, so that the dashboard shows the forecast live (Section~\ref{sec:dashboard}); it is labelled experimental because of the offline results below.
\end{itemize}

Two simple baselines were used for comparison: ``depth stays as it is now'', and a straight-line extrapolation of the last minute.

\subsubsection{Dashboard}

It has four screens: \emph{Dashboard} (titled Live Overview), \emph{Active Alerts}, \emph{Historical Reports} and \emph{System Administration}. The Live Overview polls the interface every three seconds and shows the following.

\begin{itemize}

The LSTM forecaster's depth error was 1.24~cm (root mean square), against 1.23~cm for the ``stays as now'' baseline and 2.86~cm for straight-line extrapolation. Its time-to-Critical estimate was off by 2.4 minutes on average, which is no better than guessing a constant remaining time of about 2.8 minutes (a mean error of about 1.7 minutes). \subsubsection{Discussion}

The Random Forest met the accuracy and classification criteria, but the margin on accuracy is small, so the result should be read as meeting the criterion on this data set and not as a general guarantee. Detection was reliable because the conditions change flow and depth features within seconds of the onset. The lead-time criterion was only partly met: the median warning of 4.2 minutes is below five minutes, and warnings of at least five minutes occurred on three of the six trials in which the event took that long.

The Isolation Forest was less reliable because the Normal channel with flow produced flickering depth readings that overlap with early event readings. Gating the Random Forest with the Isolation Forest therefore removed true detections without removing false ones.

The LSTM forecaster did not outperform simple baselines. The data contain ten short events that rise suddenly within three to ten minutes of the onset, and the readings before the onset carry almost no trend, so there is little to learn a forecast from. The Random Forest probabilities as inputs raised the number of warned trials from one to between three and five across runs, but the run-to-run variation (three to five trials) shows that this result is not stable. The pipeline therefore delivers an early diagnosis and warning (Stage~2) but not a reliable forecast of the water level (Stage~3 as designed).

\subsubsection{Detection and Early Warning}

Detection and early warning are different results. A method \emph{detects} a condition when it raises an alert and names the right class at some time after the onset. It gives an \emph{early warning} only when that alert comes before the Critical level and with enough lead time to act. The Random Forest detected all ten trials and named the class correctly in all ten, but only three of its warnings came five minutes or more before the Critical press, with a median lead time of 4.2 minutes. The first result supports the claim that the pipeline can recognise the staged conditions; the second shows that it does not yet warn early enough in most cases.

The five-minute target was missed for three reasons. First, physical conditions limited the time available: in four of the ten trials the water took less than five minutes to go from the onset to the Critical level (2.93, 3.12, 4.67 and 4.94 minutes, Table~\ref{tab:critical-depth}), so a five-minute warning could not have been given in those trials. Second, the alert needs six abnormal readings in a row, about 50 seconds, which was chosen to avoid false alerts, and the readings before the onset carry almost no trend. Third, in the six trials that did allow five minutes, three warnings arrived too late or too close to the Critical press, because the early readings of a blockage or flood overlap with the flickering readings of a Normal channel with flow.

The false alerts were counted before the onset of a trial. The Random Forest raised two false alerts over the ten trials and the Isolation Forest raised four. In the Normal runs, 91\% of the readings before onset were called Normal by the Random Forest. A false alert is costly in practice because it can teach operators to ignore the siren, so the six-in-a-row rule was kept even though it delays the alert.

\subsubsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item Ten staged trials on one channel model with controlled water: results may not carry over to a real drain.
\item Only the new sensor mounting was used for the final depth models; about 23 hours of Normal baseline were recorded, against 48 planned.
\item Gas sensors were not calibrated, so gas readings are uncalibrated voltages.
\item Node~2 depth was unreliable while water was poured.
\item The flow sensor works from 1 to 30~L/min, was not calibrated against a measured volume, and its readings below 1~L/min are not reliable.
\item Critical depth was judged by eye at node~1 and varied between trials.
\item The messaging broker ran without authentication or encryption on a private hotspot, and the dashboard uses one shared administration key and no user accounts.
\item The depth models were trained with floor distances of 45.76 and 45.80~cm, and the dashboard later used 45.69 and 45.46~cm; the models were not retrained.
\item The live LSTM forecast is experimental. It was not better than the baselines offline and was checked live only on a dry channel and on recorded trials.
\item A blockage run on the live dashboard \pending{state whether the Random Forest raised an alert in the recorded blockage, and why not if it did not}.
\item The system was deployed locally on a laptop and was not hosted publicly.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions, Recommendations and Future Works}
\label{ch:conclusions}

\subsection{Introduction}

This chapter answers the objectives and research questions of Chapter~1 from the evidence reported in Chapter~5. Conclusions state what the study established, recommendations propose action that follows from the findings, and future works name research that remains open. No new results are introduced.

\subsection{Conclusions}

Urban flooding in Nairobi is made worse by blocked drains and by monitoring that reacts after the water has risen. This study built a prototype to monitor a drain continuously and to warn of blockage and flood: two ESP32 nodes measuring water level, flow and gas voltages, a message broker and a database on a laptop, a three-stage machine-learning pipeline, and a web dashboard with an alert popup and siren. It was tested on ten staged trials on a channel model. The conclusions follow the order of the objectives.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*.]
\item \textbf{Challenges of existing practice.} The literature review showed that drainage management in Kenya depends on manual inspection and on responses after flooding, with little real-time monitoring (Chapter~2). This established the need that the prototype addresses.
\item \textbf{Existing tools and their limitations.} The review of related systems found sensing and prediction systems that either lacked a deployable low-cost design or did not report how early they warned, which is the gap that the evaluation of lead time addresses.
\item \textbf{Sensor-based framework.} The two-node framework collected and stored depth, flow and gas readings about every 8.5 seconds and displayed them within the dashboard refresh interval. The dry-floor distance repeated within 0.05~cm at both nodes. Its recovery from database and backend outages was tested, and the tests of stale-data detection and of the flow sensor are reported in Table~\ref{tab:iot-tests} \pending{state in one line the outcome of TC-06 and TC-07}. The gas sensors and the flow sensor were not calibrated, so their readings are voltages and uncalibrated estimates.
\item \textbf{Machine-learning pipeline.} The Random Forest detected all ten staged trials and named the correct class in all ten, with 81\% of the readings during events classified correctly. The Isolation Forest alone detected four trials and, used as a gate, reduced detection, so it is shown beside the diagnosis and does not control the alert. The LSTM forecaster did not improve on simple baselines, and it is shown on the dashboard as an experimental indicator only. The pipeline therefore supports detection and classification of the staged conditions, but not a reliable forecast of the water level.
\item \textbf{Performance and reliability.} Against the acceptance criteria, the accuracy target was met narrowly, the correct class was given at the first alert in all trials, and the five-minute warning was given in three of ten trials with a median lead time of 4.2 minutes. Four trials reached the Critical level in less than five minutes from the onset, so a five-minute warning was not possible in them. The two Random Forest false alerts and the four of the Isolation Forest show that false alerts remain a limitation.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Hypothesis.} The hypothesis combined a classification accuracy of at least 80\% with a warning at least five minutes before the Critical level. The first part was met and the second was not met in most trials, so the hypothesis is partially supported.

\textbf{Contribution and boundaries.} The study contributes a low-cost, locally deployed prototype with a documented dataset of ten staged trials and an honest comparison of three model types for detecting and warning of drainage conditions. The conclusions hold only for a controlled channel model with two nodes, water from a pump and a bucket, and a laptop deployment. They do not show performance on a live Nairobi drain, in rain, with debris, or over long periods.

\subsection{Recommendations}

The recommendations are ordered by priority.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Essential safeguards, for anyone who builds on the prototype.} Use the system only as a laboratory or pilot aid with a person watching, and treat an alert as a prompt to inspect the drain and not as a decision. This follows from the 3-in-10 early-warning result and from the false alerts.
\item \textbf{Before any use beyond the private network (developers and maintainers).} Add a user name, password and encryption to the message broker, replace the shared administration key with user accounts, and keep all credentials out of the repository. This follows from the open broker and the single shared key (Section~\ref{sec:dashboard}) and from the exposure of credentials in earlier commits.
\item \textbf{Data collection (researchers and developers).} Record more staged and real events, with depth rising at different speeds, before trusting a classifier or a forecaster. This follows from the ten trials and from the unstable forecaster results, which varied between three and five warned trials across runs.
\item \textbf{Calibration (developers).} Calibrate the flow sensor against a measured volume and the gas sensors against a reference, and measure the dry floor again after any remounting. This follows from the datasheet flow factor, the uncalibrated gas voltages and the floor change of Section~\ref{sec:hardware}.
\item \textbf{Use of the forecast (users).} Do not rely on the LSTM forecast for decisions. It matched but did not beat the baseline that assumes the level stays as it is.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Future Works}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Forecasting with more data.} The forecaster could not learn from ten short events with almost no trend before the onset. Future work could collect a larger set of events, including slow rises, and compare other forecasting methods with the same baselines and leave-one-trial-out validation.
\item \textbf{Field trial.} Testing the nodes on a real drain would show the effect of rain, debris, temperature and power on the readings, and whether the five-minute warning can be given when the water rises more slowly than in the laboratory.
\item \textbf{Hardware revision.} The depth of node~2 was unreliable while water was poured. A second sensor type or a different mounting could be compared with the ultrasonic sensor, and the power use and wireless range of the nodes could be measured for outdoor use.
\item \textbf{Alert policy.} The effect of requiring fewer or more than six abnormal readings in a row could be studied with a larger Normal data set, to balance early warning against false alerts.
\end{enumerate}
